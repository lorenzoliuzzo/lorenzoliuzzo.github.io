The integers under addition, the rotations of a square and the invertible matrices look unrelated. They share four rules, and everything that follows from those rules holds for all of them at once.

# Groups

> A **group** is a set $G$ with an operation $\cdot$ that combines two elements into one, such that:
>
> 1. the operation is **closed**: $a\cdot b\in G$ for all $a,b\in G$;
> 2. it is **associative**: $(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)$;
> 3. there is an **identity** $e$ with $e\cdot a=a\cdot e=a$ for all $a$;
> 4. every $a$ has an **inverse** $a^{-1}$ with $a\cdot a^{-1}=a^{-1}\cdot a=e$.
{: .definition #def-group title="Group"}

Commutativity is not required. A group is **abelian** if it also satisfies $a\cdot b=b\cdot a$.

> In every group the identity is unique, each element has exactly one inverse, and $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$, with the order reversed.
{: .property #prop-group-uniqueness title="Uniqueness of identity and inverses"}

> If $e$ and $e'$ are both identities, then $e=ee'=e'$. If $b$ and $c$ both invert $a$, then $b=b(ac)=(ba)c=c$. For the last claim, $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1})=a(bb^{-1})a^{-1}=e$.
{: .proof}

The motivating examples are symmetries: the set of ways to move an object back onto itself, composed, is a group, with "do nothing" as the identity and "undo" as the inverse. Others come from numbers.

A finite group is described completely by its **Cayley table**, with the product $ab$ in row $a$ and column $b$. Every row and column contains each element exactly once, because $ax=ay$ implies $x=y$. In the table of $\mathbb Z_6$ the entries are symmetric about the diagonal; in the table for the six symmetries of a triangle they are not, since rotating and then reflecting differs from reflecting and then rotating.

# Subgroups

A subset $H\subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** if it is a group under the same operation. It suffices to check that $H$ is non-empty and that $a,b\in H$ implies $ab^{-1}\in H$. The even integers form a subgroup of $\mathbb Z$, the rotations form a subgroup of the symmetries of the triangle, and the matrices of determinant $1$ form a subgroup of the invertible matrices, since $\det(AB^{-1})=1$. The set of odd integers is not a subgroup: it does not contain $0$.

**Lagrange's theorem.** In a finite group $G$, the order $|H|$ of a subgroup divides $|G|$. The proof partitions $G$ into **cosets** $gH=\{gh:h\in H\}$. Each coset has $|H|$ elements, because $h\mapsto gh$ is one-to-one, and two cosets are either equal or disjoint. So $|G|=|H|\cdot(\text{number of cosets})$. For the six symmetries of the triangle the subgroups have order $1,2,3$ or $6$; there is none of order $4$ or $5$.

# Cyclic groups and order

The **order** of an element $a$ is the smallest $n\ge1$ with $a^n=e$, or infinity if there is none. The elements $a,a^2,\dots,a^{n}=e$ form the **cyclic subgroup** $\langle a\rangle$ generated by $a$ and its size is the order of $a$. By Lagrange the order of every element divides $|G|$. A group generated by one element is **cyclic**, and $\mathbb Z_n$ is the model: every cyclic group of order $n$ looks like it.

In $\mathbb Z_6$ the element $1$ has order $6$, so $\mathbb Z_6=\langle1\rangle$; the element $2$ has order $3$ ($2,4,0$); $3$ has order $2$; $0$ has order $1$. In general the element $k$ of $\mathbb Z_n$ has order $n/\gcd(n,k)$.

A cyclic group of order $n$ has exactly one subgroup for each divisor $d$ of $n$, namely the one generated by $n/d$ of the generator, which has order $d$. For $\mathbb Z_6$ the divisors are $1,2,3,6$, and the subgroups are $\{0\}$, $\{0,3\}$, $\{0,2,4\}$ and $\mathbb Z_6$.

> Lagrange's theorem does not have a converse in general. A group of order $12$ need not have a subgroup of order $6$, although $6$ divides $12$; the alternating group $A_4$ is the standard example.

# Recap

<details class="qa" markdown="1">
<summary>What are the group axioms?</summary>

Closure, associativity, an identity element, and an inverse for every element. Commutativity is an extra property of abelian groups.
</details>

<details class="qa" markdown="1">
<summary>What does Lagrange's theorem say, and why is it true?</summary>

$|H|$ divides $|G|$ for a subgroup $H$ of a finite group $G$, because the cosets of $H$ partition $G$ into pieces of size $|H|$.
</details>

<details class="qa" markdown="1">
<summary>What is the order of an element, and how many subgroups does $\mathbb Z_n$ have?</summary>

The smallest $n$ with $a^n=e$, which is the size of $\langle a\rangle$. $\mathbb Z_n$ has one subgroup for each divisor of $n$.
</details>
