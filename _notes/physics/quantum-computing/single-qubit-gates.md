A quantum circuit acts on a qubit with a unitary matrix. Because a qubit is a point on the Bloch sphere, each such matrix has a geometric meaning: it turns the sphere, and everything in this note follows from that.

# Unitary gates

> A **unitary gate** on a qubit is a $2\times2$ complex matrix $U$ with $U^\dagger U=I$. It maps a state $|\psi\rangle$ to $U|\psi\rangle$, preserves the norm and inner products, and is reversible, with inverse $U^\dagger$.
{: .definition #def-unitary-gate title="Unitary gate"}

Unitarity is the only requirement, since probabilities must still sum to one after the gate. Up to an unobservable global phase $e^{i\alpha}$, such a matrix is a rotation of the Bloch sphere: it moves the Bloch vector $\mathbf r=(\langle X\rangle,\langle Y\rangle,\langle Z\rangle)$ to $R\mathbf r$ for a proper rotation $R$ of three-dimensional space, and every rotation arises this way. Three real parameters fix a rotation, which is also all a $2\times2$ unitary has once the global phase is dropped.

# Rotation gates

The Pauli operators square to the identity, so exponentiating one is easy. For $P\in\{X,Y,Z\}$,
$$ e^{-i\theta P/2}=\cos\tfrac\theta2\,I-i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,P, $$
because the even powers of $P$ sum to the cosine and the odd powers to $P$ times the sine.

> The **rotation gate** about axis $P=X,Y,Z$ by angle $\theta$ is $R_P(\theta)=e^{-i\theta P/2}$. It rotates the Bloch sphere by $\theta$ about the corresponding axis, counterclockwise when looking from the positive axis towards the origin.
{: .definition #def-rotation-gate title="Rotation gate"}

The half angle is the signature of spin-1/2 geometry: a full turn $\theta=2\pi$ gives $-I$, a global phase that changes nothing observable, and the true period of the Bloch vector is $2\pi$ while the matrix has period $4\pi$. As a matrix, $R_Z(\theta)=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\theta/2},e^{i\theta/2})$, so it only changes the relative phase $\varphi$ and leaves the probabilities in the computational basis alone.

{% include fig.html src="quantum-computing/rotation-gate" id="fig-rotation-gate" alt="Graph over a full turn of three curves: a cosine starting at 1, a negative sine starting at 0, and a flat zero line." caption="The expectation values after $R_X(\theta)$ acts on $|0\rangle$. The $X$ component stays zero, and $Z$ and $Y$ turn into each other as the vector goes around the $x$ axis." %}

The same fact in the language of operators: conjugation by $R_Z(\theta)$ leaves $Z$ alone and turns $X$ into $\cos\theta\,X+\sin\theta\,Y$, which is how a rotation gate acts on the three Bloch components as a rotation matrix.

# The Hadamard gate

> The **Hadamard gate** is $H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{X+Z}{\sqrt2}$. It exchanges the computational and the $X$ bases, $H|0\rangle=|+\rangle$ and $H|1\rangle=|-\rangle$, and it is its own inverse.
{: .definition #def-hadamard-gate title="Hadamard gate"}

Since $H=(X+Z)/\sqrt2$ is a Hermitian unitary with square $I$, it equals $e^{i\pi/2}R_n(\pi)$ for the axis $\mathbf n=(1,0,1)/\sqrt2$: a half turn of the sphere about the diagonal between $x$ and $z$. A half turn swaps the poles with the $\pm x$ points, which is the basis exchange above. It also exchanges $X$ and $Z$ under conjugation, $HXH=Z$ and $HZH=X$, and sends $Y$ to $-Y$.

> Applying $H$ to $|0\rangle$ gives an equal superposition, so a measurement of the result returns $0$ or $1$ with probability $1/2$ each. This is the step that opens almost every quantum algorithm.
{: .application title="Preparing a uniform superposition"}

# Decomposing a gate

> **Euler decomposition.** Every single-qubit unitary can be written $U=e^{i\alpha}R_Z(\beta)R_Y(\gamma)R_Z(\delta)$ for real angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$.
{: .theorem #thm-euler-decomposition title="Euler decomposition of a qubit gate"}

This is the Euler-angle statement that any rotation is a rotation about $z$, then $y$, then $z$. In hardware it is why a device needs only a small set of calibrated rotations and a recipe to combine them. For the Hadamard gate, for example, applying $R_Z(\pi)$ first and $R_Y(\pi/2)$ second gives $H$ up to a global phase, as a direct multiplication confirms.

> Two gates differing by a global phase are the same gate physically, so $S$ and $R_Z(\pi/2)$ are interchangeable on one qubit. The distinction reappears once a gate is controlled by another qubit, because the phase then becomes relative.
{: .trap}

> To find what a gate does, compute its axis and angle: write it as $e^{i\alpha}(\cos\frac\theta2 I-i\sin\frac\theta2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ and read $\theta$ from the trace, $|\mathrm{tr}\,U|=2|\cos\frac\theta2|$.
{: .rule}

# Recap

<details class="qa" markdown="1">
<summary>Why is every single-qubit gate a rotation of the Bloch sphere?</summary>

A unitary preserves inner products, so it preserves the length of the Bloch vector and its angles to other vectors. The only maps with those properties are rotations, and each of them comes from a unitary.
</details>

<details class="qa" markdown="1">
<summary>Why does $R_P(\theta)$ carry a half angle?</summary>

The generator is $P/2$, the spin operator, so a rotation of the Bloch vector by $\theta$ multiplies the state vector by $e^{\mp i\theta/2}$. A full turn returns the vector to itself and the state to minus itself.
</details>

<details class="qa" markdown="1">
<summary>What does the Hadamard gate do in terms of bases?</summary>

It swaps the computational basis $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ with the $X$ basis $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$, and it exchanges $X$ with $Z$ by conjugation.
</details>
